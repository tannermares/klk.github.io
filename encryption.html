How your photos stay private

Last updated October 17, 2026

The photos, videos, captions and comments you post to klk are locked on your phone before they leave it, and only the phones of the people in that klk can unlock them. This page explains how, in plain words first. Further down, the same thing is spelled out in full for anyone who wants to check our work.

The short version

  1. Locked before it leaves your phone. Photos, videos, captions, comments and profile photos are encrypted on your phone. What travels over the internet, and what we store, is scrambled.
  2. Only your klk has the key. Each klk has its own key, and only its members' phones hold it. We keep your posts, but we have no key to open them.
  3. Our servers only ever hold scrambled data. That goes for us, for the companies whose computers we rent (Google and Cloudflare), and for anyone who breaks into them. If we were ever ordered to hand over your photos, scrambled data is all we would have to give.

A photo's trip

Here is what happens when you post a photo of the lake to your Family klk.

Think of a lockbox that only certain keys open. Your phone puts the photo in the box and locks it with the Family klk's key, then uploads the locked box. Our servers keep it and pass it along to the other members' phones. Their phones have the key, so they unlock it and show the photo. Everywhere in between, the photo is noise: random-looking bytes that are useless without the key.

Every klk has its own key

A klk gets its own key the moment it is created. The creator's phone makes it from random numbers, and it is never stored anywhere in a form we could use.

To hand that key out, each person also has a personal key that lives on their phone. Every member gets their own copy of the klk's key, locked with their personal key, so we can deliver it to them without being able to use it ourselves.

If you are in two klks, your phone holds two keys. Jo, who is only in the Cabin trip klk with you, holds only that one, so nothing posted in Family can be opened on Jo's phone.

How someone new gets the key

When you invite someone, klk makes a link that looks like this:

Everything after the # is the part of a web address that browsers and apps never send to a website's server. That is a rule of how the web works, not just a promise of ours. The one-time key travels from your phone, through whatever app you send the link with, to theirs. Once they have joined, their phone locks its own copy of the klk's key and the invite stops carrying it.

It is also why an unused invite deserves the care of a house key: whoever holds it can get in. Each one works once and expires, and you can cancel all of them from the klk's page.

When someone leaves

When someone leaves a klk or is removed, one of the remaining members' phones makes a brand-new key and gives it to everyone still in the klk. New posts are locked with the new key, which the person who left never receives.

Encryption can't take back what someone has already seen. Posts from before they left stay readable to them, the same way a photo they saved stays saved.

Your recovery passphrase

Your personal key lives in your phone's secure storage (the Keychain on iPhone, the Keystore on Android). Lose the phone and you would lose the key with it, and with it every klk. So when you set up klk, you choose a recovery passphrase.

Your phone uses the passphrase to lock a copy of your personal key, and we store that locked copy. On a new phone, you type the passphrase and it unlocks the copy. The passphrase itself never leaves your phone.

This is why we can't reset it. There is no master key and no back door. If there were one, we could use it to see your photos. Choose something you will remember and nobody could guess; a short sentence works well.

What isn't encrypted

Encryption hides what you share, not the fact that you shared it. To deliver posts and notifications, our servers still see some things: your email and display name, which klks you are in, and who posted, liked, viewed or commented when. The privacy policy keeps the complete list, under What we can still see.

Notifications say who did something, never what they said or shared. The server has nothing readable to put in them.

The one time we see something you posted is when someone shows it to us. If you report a post, your phone sends us its caption and the key to just its photos, so we can review it. Nothing else in the klk is opened.

Geek stuff

The full details

Everything above, stated precisely, for anyone who builds or audits this kind of thing. klk is built from standard, openly reviewed primitives, combined in standard ways. None of the cryptography is invented here.

Building blocks

Job What klk uses
Captions, comments, key copies, the passphrase-locked key XChaCha20-Poly1305 with a random 192-bit nonce
Photo and video files, and their thumbnails libsodium's crypto_secretstream_xchacha20poly1305, in 1 MiB chunks
Giving a member their copy of a klk key X25519 with a fresh ephemeral key per copy, HKDF-SHA256 to derive the wrapping key, then XChaCha20-Poly1305
Turning your passphrase into a key Argon2id: 19 MiB of memory, 2 passes, parallelism 1 (OWASP's recommendation), 16-byte random salt
Randomness The operating system's secure random number generator
Libraries @noble/ciphers, curves and hashes (audited) for keys and text; libsodium on the device for files (pinned on iOS, lazysodium on Android); the reference Argon2 implementation, natively

Every key is 256 bits. The Argon2id parameters are stored next to each passphrase-locked key, so they can be raised later without locking anyone out.

The key hierarchy

  1. Recovery passphrase Only in your head.
  2. Argon2id, with a random salt
  3. Escrow key Derived on your phone when needed, never stored.
  4. XChaCha20-Poly1305, bound to your account id
  5. Identity key (X25519) The secret half lives in the Keychain or Keystore, and we store a copy locked by the escrow key. The public half is on your profile, and the security rules stop it changing once it is set.
  6. Sealed box, bound to the klk, the key version and you
  7. klk content key One per klk per version, with a random 128-bit version id. Each member holds their own sealed copy.
  8. XChaCha20-Poly1305
  9. Captions and comments Stored as klk1:<version>:<ciphertext>. File keys A fresh one for every photo or video. It seals the file in Cloudflare R2 and a 360px thumbnail kept on the post.

A file gets its own key, rather than the klk's, so the native code that handles bulk bytes only ever sees a key that opens one file, and a report can hand over one photo without exposing the klk. Profile photos work the same way: the photo has its own key, sealed under the current key of each klk you share with someone, plus one copy sealed to you.

}

Every sealed file starts with the same nine bytes: KLKM, a format version and the chunk size. That is the S0xLTQIAABAA at the front of the thumbnail, in base64. After it, there is only ciphertext.

What the design guards against

  • Ciphertext moved to the wrong place. Each sealed thing is bound to where it belongs, as authenticated data. A klk key copy names its klk, its version and its recipient. A file key can't be opened as a caption, or a caption as a file key. A profile photo is bound to its owner, and the passphrase-locked key to your account. Move one and it fails to open instead of showing up somewhere it shouldn't.
  • Planted content. There is no plaintext fallback. If a caption or photo on the server isn't properly sealed, the app shows nothing, and the security rules refuse to store it in the first place. Nobody with database access, us included, can plant a caption or photo that appears to come from a member.
  • Forged key requests. A member whose phone is missing a klk's key can ask the others for it. The request names only who is asking. Other members' phones check that person is on the klk's member list and seal the key to the public key on their profile, so a forged request can't send the key anywhere else.
  • Cut or shuffled files. secretstream chains each chunk to the one before it and marks the last one, so a file with chunks dropped, reordered or cut short won't open. The header is pushed through the stream too, so a doctored version or chunk size is caught.
  • Open storage. The R2 buckets have no public access. Your phone asks a server function for a link, which checks that you are in the klk and signs a link to that one file, valid for an hour. Uploads go to a name the server chooses, signed for the exact number of bytes. The app only fetches from our own bucket, so a forged post can't make your phone contact someone else's server and reveal that you looked.
  • Hidden details in the file. Before encrypting, your phone re-encodes photos, which removes their EXIF data, location included. Videos are re-encoded without metadata unless they are already small and carry no location.
  • Leaks around the edges. Push notifications carry a title and nothing else. Crash reports never include decrypted text: the app drops the tap and console records that could otherwise carry what was on screen.

What it doesn't protect against

  • People in the klk. Members can see, screenshot and save what is posted. Encryption decides who is in the room, not what they do there.
  • An unlocked phone. Whoever has your phone and its passcode has your klks.
  • A guessable passphrase. We store the passphrase-locked copy of your key. Argon2id makes every guess slow and memory-hungry, but a passphrase from a list of common passwords could still be guessed by someone holding a copy of our database.
  • A server altered to lie. klk doesn't yet let you compare keys with another member, the way Signal's safety numbers do, so your phone trusts our server about who is in a klk and which public key belongs to whom. Someone who took control of the server, us included, could add a member who isn't real, which everyone would see in the klk's member list. Or they could swap a member's public key for their own, which nobody would see. Either way, they would receive what is posted after that. Content already stored stays sealed. This takes deliberate tampering, and key verification is what would close it.
  • Moving a caption within a klk. Captions and comments are bound to their klk and key version, not to the individual post, and authorship isn't signed. So someone with database access could move a caption from one post to another in the same klk. They couldn't read it or write a new one.
  • The app itself. As with any encrypted app, you are trusting that the app on your phone does what this page says. klk's source code is not public.

Things you can check yourself

  • The invite page. If klk isn't installed, an invite opens klkapp.com/join, a static page. View its source: it reads the invite from the fragment, clears it from the address bar, sends no referrer, and passes the invite only to the klk app.
  • The traffic. Put klk's traffic through a debugging proxy and every photo or video upload starts with the bytes KLKM and is noise after that. Captions and comments go up as klk1:… tokens.
  • The notifications. Every push klk sends says who did something, never what they said or shared.
